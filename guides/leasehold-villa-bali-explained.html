Leasehold villas in Bali explained

If you are a foreign buyer looking at a villa in Bali, you will almost certainly be looking at a leasehold villa. Leasehold is the most common way foreigners hold a villa in Bali, and it is how our own villas in Munggu are offered: leasehold 30 years + renewal. This guide explains what villa leasehold in Bali means, how leasehold compares with freehold, and what to check in a lease before you sign.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules can change. Always use an independent notary and lawyer in Bali before you pay.

What a leasehold villa in Bali is

With a leasehold (Hak Sewa), you lease the land and the villa on it for a fixed number of years. The lease is a contract between you and the holder of the land rights, and it is signed before a notary. For the length of the lease, the villa is yours to live in, to use as a holiday home or, where the zoning and permits allow it, to rent out.

When the term ends, the right goes back to the landowner unless the lease is extended or renewed. That is why the renewal terms are one of the most important parts of the contract.

A leasehold can be held in your own name, and it does not require a visa or a residence permit. That simplicity is a big part of why most foreign buyers choose it.

Leasehold vs freehold in Bali

Freehold land in Indonesia (Hak Milik) can only be held by Indonesian citizens. A foreigner cannot hold Hak Milik, so when you compare leasehold vs freehold in Bali as a foreign buyer, the real comparison is between leasehold and the other legal routes open to foreigners:

  • Leasehold (Hak Sewa): a lease for a fixed term, in your own name, signed before a notary. No residence permit needed.
  • Right to use (Hak Pakai): a registered right that a foreigner living in Indonesia with a KITAS or KITAP can hold for a home.
  • A foreign-owned company (PT PMA) with HGB: a company with foreign shareholders holds a right to build (Hak Guna Bangunan). It suits people who run a business, and it comes with company obligations.

Freehold "through a friend" is not one of the options. A nominee arrangement, where land is put in an Indonesian person's name on your behalf, is risky: it is not recognised by Indonesian law, and buyers can lose the property. 30 years + renewal: how the term works

Psusennes Villas are offered on a leasehold of 30 years + renewal. Three questions decide how much those years are worth to you.

When do the 30 years start?

For an off-plan villa, ask whether the lease period starts when you sign or when the villa is handed over. Our villas are under construction, with completion planned for February 2027, so this is a question worth putting in writing.

What exactly does the renewal say?

"Renewal" can mean different things in different contracts. Read the clause for the length of the renewal, how its price is set, and whether it is an option you can exercise or something that still has to be negotiated.

What happens at the end?

Check what the contract says about the land and the building when the term and any renewal come to an end, so there are no surprises later.

What to check in a Bali villa lease

  • The land: your notary should check the land certificate, the owner, the zoning and the building permit (PBG).
  • The parties: who signs the lease, and whether they hold the right to lease the land for the full term.
  • The start date and the length of the lease, written clearly.
  • The renewal clause: its length, its price or formula, and its conditions.
  • Transfer: whether you can transfer the remaining lease to a new buyer, and on what conditions.
  • Inheritance: whether the lease passes to your heirs.
  • Use and rental: whether you may rent the villa out, and whether the zoning and permits allow it.
  • Taxes and fees: ask your notary to list everything you pay on top of the price.

A notary (PPAT) prepares and records the lease deed. Use your own independent notary and lawyer, and do not rely only on the seller's.
