Buyer guide

Can foreigners buy property in Bali?

Short answer: yes, but not as freehold in your own name. In Indonesia, freehold land (Hak Milik) is reserved for Indonesian citizens. Foreign buyers hold Bali property through other legal structures, and the most common one for a villa is a long leasehold. This guide explains the options in plain language, the steps of a purchase and what to check before you sign.

This is general information, not legal advice. Rules and thresholds can change. Always use an independent notary and lawyer in Bali before you pay.

The three ways foreigners hold property in Bali

1. Leasehold (Hak Sewa)

You lease the land and villa for a fixed term, often 25 to 30 years, under a lease signed before a notary. It is the simplest route, it can be held in your own name, and it does not require a visa or residence permit. Many leases include an option to extend. Its value depends on the quality of the lease contract, so the extension terms matter as much as the price.

2. Right to use (Hak Pakai)

Hak Pakai is a registered right that foreigners who live in Indonesia with a valid stay permit (KITAS or KITAP) can hold for a residential property. It runs for a long initial term and can be extended. It is usually limited to one home per person and is subject to minimum price levels set by region.

3. A foreign-owned company (PT PMA) with HGB

A PT PMA is an Indonesian company with foreign shareholders. It can hold a right to build (Hak Guna Bangunan, HGB) for 30 years, with extension and renewal. This suits investors who run a rental business or develop property, but it comes with company set-up, capital and reporting obligations.

What to avoid

Do not use a nominee, meaning freehold land put in the name of an Indonesian friend or partner on your behalf. These arrangements are not recognised by Indonesian law and buyers can lose the property.

How a leasehold villa purchase works

  1. Choose the villa and check the price, term and renewal terms in writing.
  2. Due diligence. Your notary checks the land certificate, the owner, zoning and the building permit (PBG).
  3. Reservation and agreement. For an off-plan villa, the agreement sets the payment schedule, the completion date and what happens if it is late.
  4. Notarial lease deed. The lease is signed before a notary (PPAT) and the lease period starts.
  5. Handover. You inspect the finished villa against the plans and material schedule, then receive the keys.

What to check before you sign

  • When does the lease period start: at signing or at handover? For off-plan villas this matters.
  • The extension or renewal clause: the length, the price or formula, and whether it is guaranteed.
  • Whether you can sell or transfer the lease, and pass it to your heirs.
  • The zoning of the land and whether you may rent the villa out.
  • The building permit and the construction progress, ideally seen in person or on regular site updates.
  • All taxes and fees on top of the price, which your notary can list for you.
